Introduction

Slow replies cost small training businesses clients.

Parents message coaches at night and wait hours for answers about price, packages, and scheduling. By the time the coach replies, many have moved on.

In my first AI lab, I used design thinking to build the GK Coach Assistant, a no-code chatbot that answers those questions instantly, recommends the right package, and collects booking details for me to confirm.

Description

A four-part AI lab, ending in a working bot.

  • LLM prompt testing. I ran one structured prompt (Context / Task / Constraints) asking for a 4-week goalkeeper training plan in three separate ChatGPT chats, then graded each on five criteria. All three broke the same formatting rule, which showed me hard rules must be stated forcefully and tested.
  • Custom GPT practice. I used a GPT Store writing assistant to draft Instagram captions for my training business. Adding my own background was the single biggest improvement.
  • Research assistant evaluation. I tested Stanford's STORM on the evolution of the sweeper-keeper and fact-checked it with my D1 experience. It was well organized but placed Neuer's rise in the wrong decade and cited the wrong sources for several claims.
  • Bot build. I designed, built, and tested the GK Coach Assistant in Chatbase, using only my own business information as its knowledge base.
Objective

Learn to build responsible AI that solves a real problem.

My objective was to learn how to design, build, and test a responsible AI chatbot for a real business need. The problem statement I designed around:

How might we give parents and players instant, accurate answers about private GK training, at any hour, so they can book without waiting for the coach?

Process

Design thinking, step by step.

  1. Map the users Empathize

    Parents need price, packages, schedule, and coach credentials. Players want to know if training fits their level. I lose time on repeat questions and lose leads to slow replies. Many players are minors, so ethics shaped the design from day one.

  2. Frame the problem Define

    I wrote the "How might we" statement above and set limits: the bot can answer, recommend, and collect details, but can't see my calendar or take payment.

  3. Compare four AI ideas Ideate

    An FAQ and booking bot, a drill generator, video technique analysis, and lead prediction. I chose the FAQ and booking bot: it solved the defined problem and could be built today with a free tool.

  4. Build the bot Prototype

    I loaded a knowledge base (credentials, prices, packages, policies, how to book) and wrote instructions with a role, goal, conversation steps, and strict rules: no made-up info, no bookings or payments, no medical advice, no promised results, and a parent's contact for any player under 18.

  5. Test every rule Test

    I wrote one test message per rule and ran each in a fresh chat, so no earlier conversation could influence the answer.

  6. Fix and retest Iterate

    Version 2 fixed two problems. Retesting surfaced a third, now planned for version 3.

Value proposition

Why this matters.

Unique value

This bot was built by a coach for his own business, not as a classroom exercise. My experience as a D1 goalkeeper and GK coach let me define the real problem, write accurate content, and catch AI mistakes a non-expert would miss.

Relevance

Private coaches, academies, and clubs all face the same problem: unanswered questions cost bookings. This project shows a free, no-code path any coach can follow, plus the safety rules needed when working with youth athletes.

Build your own in an afternoon

  1. List your top 10 questions. Pull them from your texts and DMs. That's your knowledge base.
  2. Write one page of facts. Prices, packages, policies, credentials, how to book. Nothing else.
  3. Give it rules before personality. No guessing, no bookings or payments, no medical advice, no promises, and a parent's contact for minors.
  4. Test one rule per chat. Write a message designed to break each rule and run it in a fresh conversation.
  5. Fix one thing at a time. Changing one instruction can shift another behavior, so retest after every change.
